# Design Thinking Frameworks

A curated library of design-thinking frameworks. The coach surfaces these at the
moment they are relevant — never as a checklist, always as a prompt.

## Empathy Map
<!-- meta: framework=Empathy Map; pillar=values; phase=empathy; industry=generic -->
An empathy map captures what a specific user **says, thinks, does, and feels**. Build it
from real observation and interviews, not from imagination. The gap between what people
*say* and what they *do* is usually where the real insight lives. Use it early, before any
solution exists, to force the team to ground its assumptions in a real person. A red flag:
an empathy map filled in from inside the building with no field contact — that is a list of
guesses, not evidence.

## Customer Journey Map
<!-- meta: framework=Journey Map; pillar=process; phase=empathy; industry=generic -->
A journey map lays out the end-to-end experience a user has with a service over time —
stages, actions, touchpoints, and the emotional highs and lows at each step. Its power is
revealing the moments that matter: the points of friction, drop-off, and delight that a
feature-by-feature view hides. Map the current ("as-is") journey from real evidence before
designing the future ("to-be") one. The most valuable output is usually the list of pain
points nobody had named before.

## Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)
<!-- meta: framework=JTBD; pillar=values; phase=define; industry=generic -->
Jobs To Be Done reframes a problem around the progress a person is trying to make, not the
product they use. The canonical form: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can
[expected outcome]." It pulls the team away from "what features should we add" toward "what
job is the user hiring this for." Strong JTBD statements are solution-agnostic — if your job
statement names your product, you have written a feature request, not a job.

## How Might We (HMW)
<!-- meta: framework=How Might We; pillar=process; phase=define; industry=generic -->
"How Might We" turns a defined problem or insight into an open, optimistic question that
invites ideas. "Users abandon checkout when surprised by shipping cost" becomes "How might
we make total cost feel predictable before checkout?" Pitch the question at the right
altitude: too broad ("HMW redesign the app") and it is paralysing; too narrow ("HMW add a
shipping line") and it smuggles the solution in. Good HMWs come straight out of a sharp
problem definition.

## Point of View (POV) Statement
<!-- meta: framework=POV Statement; pillar=process; phase=define; industry=generic -->
A POV statement crystallises who you are designing for, what they need, and the insight
behind it: "[User] needs [need] because [surprising insight]." It is the bridge from
empathy to ideation — it locks the team onto one well-understood problem before solutions
start flying. If the team cannot write a POV they all agree on, they have not finished
defining the problem and should not be ideating yet.

## Crazy 8s
<!-- meta: framework=Crazy 8s; pillar=process; phase=ideate; industry=generic -->
Crazy 8s is a fast divergence exercise: fold a sheet into eight frames and sketch eight
distinct ideas in eight minutes, one per minute. The time pressure is the point — it pushes
people past their first obvious idea (which everyone has) into less defended territory.
Quantity over polish. Run it after a clear HMW, and follow it with a convergence step so the
breadth actually narrows to something testable.

## Assumption Mapping
<!-- meta: framework=Assumption Mapping; pillar=behavior; phase=define; industry=generic -->
List every assumption a chosen direction depends on, then plot each on two axes: how
**important** it is (does the idea collapse if it is wrong?) and how **certain** you are
(do you have evidence?). The assumptions that are important *and* uncertain are your riskiest
— test those first and cheapest. This is the antidote to building confidently on a guess: it
makes the riskiest leap visible before resources are committed.

## Service Blueprint
<!-- meta: framework=Service Blueprint; pillar=resources; phase=prototype; industry=generic -->
A service blueprint extends a journey map below the surface: it maps the frontstage (what
the customer sees) against the backstage (staff actions, systems, and processes that make
each step possible). It exposes where a good customer-facing intention breaks because the
operational reality cannot support it. Use it when a service involves multiple channels,
hand-offs, or back-office systems — exactly where experiences silently fall apart.

## Prototyping Fidelity Ladder
<!-- meta: framework=Prototyping; pillar=process; phase=prototype; industry=generic -->
Match prototype fidelity to the question you are asking. A paper sketch or clickable
wireframe is enough to test whether a flow makes sense; do not build production code to
learn that. Raise fidelity only as the questions get sharper. The goal of a prototype is to
*learn*, not to impress — the cheapest artifact that can kill or confirm an assumption wins.
Over-investing in fidelity early is a sign the team is seeking validation, not learning.

## Usability Testing
<!-- meta: framework=Usability Testing; pillar=success; phase=test; industry=generic -->
Usability testing puts a real prototype in front of real users and watches them attempt real
tasks — without coaching them. Five users typically surface the majority of serious issues.
The discipline is to observe behaviour, not collect opinions: what people *do* with the
prototype matters far more than whether they say they like it. This is human-led work; an AI
cannot tell you how a real user will actually behave — it can only help you plan the test and
make sense of what you saw.
